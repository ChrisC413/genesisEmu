Testing sound without listening to it

An audio regression test that's exact, cheap and independent of the host's sound card: hash every native sample since power-on, and check the hash at scripted checkpoints.

genesisEmu build notes4 min readStory M05-06

The final story of the audio milestone answers a practical question. Once sound works, how do you notice when a later change breaks it? Listening to three title themes after every commit isn't realistic, and a listener wouldn't notice a subtle change anyway. M05-06 added two tools: WAV recording for when you do want to listen, and audio hashes for automated tests.

Hash the native samples, not the speakers

The key design choice is where to hash. The audio that reaches the sound card has passed through the resampler and dynamic rate control from M05-01, which depend on the host's sound card, its buffer and real-time timing, so they're not deterministic. One step earlier, the samples the emulated chips produce at the native ~53,267 Hz rate depend only on the emulated machine, and the machine is deterministic.

So the sound system feeds every native sample it renders into a running XXH3 hash, from power-on:

That hash covers the entire soundtrack so far, every sample of FM, DAC and PSG output after mixing. Change one sample anywhere and the hash changes. It costs one hashing pass over data that's being produced anyway.

Hash before the resampler, and the test doesn't depend on whose sound card it runs on.

A new checkpoint kind

The scripted-input format from M04-03 already had frame_hash and state_hash checkpoints. M05-06 added a third, audio_hash, with the same rules: evaluated after N frames, optional expected value, recorded with --update-script, checked with --check. Every script in tests/scripts/ now asserts it twice, at the title screen and at the end of the run:

The first one, at frame 600, covers the "SEGA" chant (M05-04) and the start of the title music. The second covers gameplay: Sonic running, jumping and getting hit. The [rom] test that replays the scripts checks these along with the frame and state hashes, so a regression in any sound chip, the mixer or the timing of driver writes fails the test.

The state hash (M02-05) also includes the audio hash, so even a state_hash checkpoint fails if the sound diverged.

WAV recording

When a hash does change, the next step is to listen. gen_cli run --wav file.wav records the same native-rate mix, 16-bit stereo at about 53,267 Hz, in a standard RIFF/WAVE file. The writer is under seventy lines: a header with placeholder sizes, the samples, and sizes patched in when the file is closed. In the app, the Sound panel (M05-05) starts and stops recording, so you can capture exactly the passage you're looking at. Recording the native mix rather than the speaker output means a WAV made by the CLI and one made in the app from the same run contain the same samples.

All the measurements quoted in the earlier audio articles, such as the −23 dBFS SEGA chant, its zero-crossing rate and the absence of clipped samples, were taken from WAVs recorded this way. As always, those recordings of commercial games stayed as scratch files and were never committed.

One box open

The acceptance criterion is title-screen audio hash stable across runs and operating systems. Across runs it holds: the hashes were recorded in one process and re-checked in another on Windows. Across operating systems it hasn't been tested, because no Mac was available. The mixer uses floating-point filters, so equality across compilers has to be demonstrated rather than assumed. So the story reads "Done on Windows; macOS run pending", and the box stays unchecked until a Mac produces the same hash.

That's where the series stands at the end of M05: a hand-written 68000 and Z80, a complete VDP, sound, and a test suite that checks millions of CPU vectors on every push and golden frames and audio hashes against the user's own ROMs, with every unverified claim marked as unverified. The next milestones turn the emulator into a workbench: a debugger, a colour-coded map of the ROM and, eventually, a reassemblable disassembly of each game.
