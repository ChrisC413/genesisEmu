M05 · Audio · Story 5 of 6

A mixing desk for reverse engineers

Mute and solo every channel, and log every sound-chip register write with the frame, scanline and program counter that made it. These are the tools for taking a sound driver apart.

genesisEmu build notes4 min readStory M05-05

For a player, audio just has to sound right. For a reverse engineer, it's something to take apart. Which channel plays the bass line? Which code writes the key-on for the jump sound? How does the driver lay out its song data? The project's later goals include moving a special stage from one Sonic game into another (M11), and its music has to keep working through a different game's sound driver. M05-05 built the tools for that kind of investigation.

Eleven channels, each mutable

The sound system treats the hardware as eleven channels: FM 1–6, the DAC, PSG tones 1–3 and PSG noise. A bit mask selects which are heard. FM and DAC bits go to the masked YM2612 output stage from M05-03; PSG bits go to the PSG mixer:

The Sound panel (Emulation > Sound...) shows a Mute and a Solo checkbox per channel. Solo works the usual way: if any channel is soloed, only soloed channels play. One rule comes from the hardware rather than the UI. The DAC replaces FM channel 6 when enabled, so muting FM 6 also silences the DAC. That's the first acceptance criterion, and a unit test checks it.

Levels and the "model 1" filter

The panel also has an output volume, separate FM and PSG levels, and a model 1 low-pass toggle. The first model of the console filters its audio output, softening the sound. The emulator approximates that with a first-order low-pass at about 3.4 kHz. It's on by default, and can be switched off for an unfiltered mix, which is often clearer when analysing individual channels:

Every register write is logged with the program counter that made it. That's how you find the sound driver's routines without reading its code first.

The register log

The more powerful tool is the register-write log, introduced in M04-07 and completed here. Every write to the YM2612 or PSG, from either CPU, is recorded with its position in time and its origin:

Entries go into a ring buffer of 65,536 (the newest writes win), with a running total so it's clear when older entries have been overwritten. For the PSG, which has no register addresses on its bus, the log records the register most recently latched, so a data byte is attributed to the right channel. The Sound panel shows the log as a table, newest first, with Clear and Export CSV buttons; the CSV goes into the ROM's user-data folder. Headless, gen_cli run --reg-log file.csv exports the last 65,536 writes.

What the log revealed

The second criterion asked for a log of 10 seconds of title music containing the expected key-on writes with Z80 PCs. Sonic 2's title (frames 540–1140) produced 226 key-on/off writes to register $28, the YM2612's key register, and every one came from the Z80 at the same PC, $0021. That single address is the driver's FM write routine. The values, such as $01, $02 and $04, are key-offs for individual channels. Without reading a line of the driver's code, the log had already identified one of its central routines.

That's the kind of result the project is built to produce: let instrumentation point at the interesting code, then read it. The planned debugger (M06) and ROM map (M07) will use the same approach for the rest of each game.

The rest of the panel

The panel also starts and stops WAV recording (M05-06) and shows the dynamic rate control status from M05-01: queue level and current ratio adjustment, which is useful when checking for underruns. This story is "Done": both of its criteria are verified by a unit test and by the logged Sonic 2 title, with no listening required.
