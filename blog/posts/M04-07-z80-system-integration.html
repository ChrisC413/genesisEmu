Two CPUs sharing one machine

With the Z80 core complete, it had to join the running console: run in step with the 68000, stop when the 68000 takes its bus, reach into 68000 memory through a bank window, and take an interrupt every frame.

genesisEmu build notes4 min readStory M04-07

By M04-05 the Z80 core passed every test in isolation. M04-07 put it into the machine, where it shares a bus with the 68000, gets stopped and reset by it, reads 68000 memory through a window, and is interrupted by the video chip. This is where the sound drivers of the three games started actually running.

Catching up

The scheduler from M02-05 had left an empty catchUp() hook for this. The 68000 still drives time line by line. At the end of each line, the Z80 runs until its clock reaches the line's end in master clocks, at 15 master clocks per T-state:

Line granularity isn't always fine enough. When the 68000 writes BUSREQ or RESET, it's usually in the middle of a handshake with the sound driver, and the Z80 must have run up to exactly that moment, not to the end of the previous line. So the bus calls a sync hook before applying either write, and the Z80 catches up to the 68000's current time first. Without that, a game waiting for the driver to acknowledge something could see a stale answer, or hang.

Tighter arbitration

M02-03 had granted the Z80 bus to the 68000 immediately and ignored the reset line, a deliberate shortcut while the Z80 didn't run. M04-07 replaced it with the real rule: the Z80 runs only while neither BUSREQ nor RESET is asserted, and the 68000 owns the Z80 bus only while BUSREQ is asserted and RESET is released. Asserting RESET also resets the Z80 CPU. A unit test checks that BUSREQ isn't granted while the Z80 is held in reset.

The Z80 can look into 68000 memory through a 32 KB window. It costs both CPUs time, and the emulator charges it to both.

The bank window

The Z80 has a 16-bit address space, but sound drivers need data from the cartridge: music, and especially drum samples. So addresses $8000–$FFFF on the Z80 side are a window into 68000 memory. A 9-bit bank register selects which 32 KB is visible, loaded one bit per write (M02-03 described the 68000's side of it).

Each window access takes over the 68000's bus, which costs time on both sides. The spec gives approximate figures, 3 Z80 wait states and 11 68000 cycles per access, and they're configurable in MachineConfig::z80Window. After each Z80 run, the 68000's clock is pushed forward by the stall it accumulated.

For reverse engineering, a window access also needs to be visible with its translated address. A read from Z80 $9234 means nothing without knowing the bank. The new onZ80Window hook reports both the Z80 address and the 68000 address it reached:

That's what the planned ROM map (M07) needs to mark cartridge bytes as "read by the Z80 as data", such as drum samples. A unit test writes the nine bank bits from Z80 code to select $FF0000, reads 68000 RAM through the window, writes back, and checks that the hook reported the translated address $FF1235.

The frame interrupt and the sound ports

The VDP holds the Z80's interrupt line for the first line of vertical blank (M03-03 exposed it). Now the Z80 actually takes it, in interrupt mode 1, once per frame, giving the driver a regular tick.

The sound chips weren't emulated yet (that's M05), but their register writes now flow somewhere useful. Every write to the YM2612 or the PSG, from either CPU, goes into a register log with the frame, line, CPU, PC, chip, port, register and value. gen_cli run --reg-log file.csv exports it.

Verified by listening without sound

The criteria were that the Sonic 2 and S3K drivers run without hanging the 68000, and that the log shows plausible YM2612 writes during title music. All three games ran over 3,000 frames of attract mode and scripted play with no faults. The register log showed the drivers at work: Sonic 1 logged 27,000 YM2612 writes in 500 frames, mostly Z80 writes to register $2A, the DAC data port, playing the "SEGA" voice sample, plus key-on events from the 68000. Sonic 2 logged 25,000. Sonic 3 & Knuckles logged over 100,000, including key-ons and PSG writes.

Running the Z80 also shifted the games' attract-mode timing by a few frames, so the boot hashes and most of the golden frame hashes from M03 changed, in a [golden-update] commit. The changed frames were compared with the cached ones: same scenes, small position shifts.
