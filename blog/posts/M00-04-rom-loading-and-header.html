Knowing exactly which ROM you're holding

Loading a cartridge dump means undoing a 1990s copier's byte shuffle, reading a 256-byte header, and fingerprinting the result.

genesisEmu build notes5 min readStory M00-04

Reverse engineering only works if you know exactly what you're looking at. "Sonic 2" isn't one program. There are several retail revisions, regional variants, compilation re-releases and countless patched dumps. A label you attach to an address in one revision may point at the middle of an instruction in another. So before genesisEmu could execute anything, it had to answer one question about a ROM file reliably: which ROM is this?

Two layouts for the same bytes

Genesis ROM dumps come in two main formats. A BIN (also .md or .gen) is the cartridge image as the CPU sees it, byte for byte. An SMD file comes from the Super Magic Drive, a copier device of the era. It has a 512-byte header, and the data is split into 16 KB blocks with the bytes inside each block shuffled.

The interesting part is deciding whether a file is SMD at all. The obvious approach, trusting the .smd extension, failed on real data: several of the user's .smd files turned out to be plain BIN images. So detection looks at content. Every Genesis image has the text SEGA at offset $100. The loader checks whether that text appears where a BIN would put it or where an SMD would scatter it, and uses the extension only to break a tie.

Several of the user's .smd files were plain images, so the extension only breaks ties.

Reading the header

Bytes $100 to $1FF of every cartridge hold a fixed-layout header: console name, copyright, domestic and overseas titles, serial number, a checksum, supported I/O devices, the ROM and RAM address ranges, an optional SRAM declaration, and region codes. parseHeader reads each field at its documented offset. Two fields are trickier than they look.

The SRAM declaration (at $1B0, the letters RA) carries a type byte that says whether the save RAM is battery-backed and whether it sits on even bytes, odd bytes or both. Sonic 3 & Knuckles saves through it, so it has to be parsed correctly.

Region codes exist in two styles. Early games list letters, J, U and E. Later games use a single hex digit as a bitmask. The parser accepts both and normalises them to one mask.

Checksums and fingerprints

The header contains a 16-bit checksum: the sum of every big-endian word from $200 to the end of the ROM. Many games check it at boot. genesisEmu computes it and compares, but a mismatch is only a warning. ROM hacks routinely have stale checksums, and refusing to load them would make the tool useless for modding.

The real identity comes from two hashes: SHA-1 for identification and CRC32 because that's what many ROM databases list. Both are implemented in-house in about a hundred lines, verified against the FIPS 180 SHA-1 test vectors and the standard CRC32 check value. The hashes are taken over the de-interleaved image, so the SMD and BIN of the same game produce the same SHA-1. A test builds a synthetic ROM, interleaves it, and checks exactly that.

A table of known games

known_roms.cpp maps SHA-1 hashes, taken from the No-Intro database, to a game, revision and region. It covers Sonic 1 REV00 and REV01, three revisions of Sonic 2, three regional Sonic 3 releases, standalone Sonic & Knuckles, and the combined "Sonic 3 & Knuckles" images.

Saying no clearly

Files under 512 bytes or over 4 MB are rejected with a clear error. The limit applies to the de-interleaved image, so a 4 MB game in SMD form, 4 MB plus a 512-byte header, still loads. Master System and Game Gear images are a common mix-up because they share file collections. The loader looks for their TMR SEGA signature and reports "Not a Mega Drive/Genesis ROM" instead of loading garbage.

Validated against real dumps

Unit tests use synthetic ROMs generated inside the test, because no commercial ROM may enter the repository. Real-world validation runs locally through tools/validate-roms.ps1, pointed at the user's own folder. On 2026-10-24 it identified Sonic 1 REV01, Sonic 2 REV01, Sonic 3 USA and Europe, and standalone Sonic & Knuckles, and the S&K + Sonic 3 pairs hashed to the known combined entries. Just as important, the negative cases behaved: a patched Sonic 2, a Sonic & Knuckles dump with three modified bytes and an unknown Sonic 1 all stayed unidentified, and .sms files were rejected.

That result settled the project's canonical ROM set (decision D-023): Sonic 1 REV01, Sonic 2 REV01, and S3K built from Sonic & Knuckles plus Sonic 3 USA.
