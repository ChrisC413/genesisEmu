Square waves and a shift register: the PSG

Next to its FM synthesizer, the Genesis has a simple sound chip inherited from the Master System. Three square waves and a noise generator, written in-house in under a hundred lines.

genesisEmu build notes4 min readStory M05-02

The Genesis has two sound chips. The famous one is the Yamaha YM2612, an FM synthesizer. The other is a PSG (programmable sound generator), a Texas Instruments SN76489-compatible block built into the VDP. It's the same kind of chip as in the Master System. It's simple: three channels of square waves and one of noise, each with a volume control. Sonic games use it for some sound effects and for parts of the music.

Decision D-013 required the CPUs to be hand-written but allowed a library for the YM2612. The PSG is simple enough that the project wrote it in-house anyway: the whole implementation is under a hundred lines.

Registers through a single port

The PSG has eight registers (a tone period and an attenuation for each of three channels, plus noise control and noise attenuation) but only one write port. A byte with bit 7 set latches a register and writes its low four bits. A byte with bit 7 clear writes the upper six bits of a tone period to the latched register. That's how a 10-bit period fits through an 8-bit port in two writes:

Both CPUs can write it: the 68000 at $C00011 and the Z80 through its window at $7F11. Writes go through the shared sound system, which first brings the chip up to the writing CPU's current time.

Tones

Each tone channel has a down-counter. The PSG clock is the Z80's (master clock ÷ 15), and the counters step once every 16 PSG clocks. When a counter reaches zero it reloads from the period register and the channel's output flips between +1 and −1, giving a square wave. One quirk is implemented explicitly: periods of 0 and 1 hold the output high instead of producing an inaudible ultrasonic tone. Software can then play samples by changing the volume register quickly.

Noise and the Sega taps

The noise channel shifts a 16-bit linear-feedback shift register (LFSR) and outputs its low bit. In "periodic" mode it just rotates a single bit, giving a buzzy tone. In "white" mode it feeds back the XOR of two bits, giving pseudo-random noise. Which bits are tapped differs between chip variants, and it changes the sound. Sega's version taps bits 0 and 3:

The noise rate is one of three fixed dividers, or it follows tone channel 3's period, which lets software set the noise pitch freely.

Which bits feed back into the noise generator differs between chip versions. Using the wrong taps gives a different-sounding hiss.

Mixing at the native rate

The PSG ticks about 223,700 times per second, much faster than the 53,267 Hz native audio rate. Each native sample covers four or five PSG ticks, and the mixer averages them (a box filter), which removes the worst aliasing from sharp square-wave edges before the resampler from M05-01 takes over.

Tests, and a listening check

Unit tests check the arithmetic that's easy to get subtly wrong:

  • Tone period: a period of $100 flips the output exactly 10 times in 10 × $100 ticks.
  • Attenuation: each step is a ratio of 0.794, and 15 is silent.
  • Noise: periodic noise repeats every 16 shifts, and white noise with Sega's taps repeats after 57,337 shifts, a value that would come out differently with other taps.

The remaining criterion, Sonic 1's ring sound effect PSG component sounds right, needs a human ear, and the story records it as open for Chris.
