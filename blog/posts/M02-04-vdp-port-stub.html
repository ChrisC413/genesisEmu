A video chip that draws nothing

Games wait on the video chip constantly. A stub that stores data, reports its status and raises the vertical interrupt on time is enough to boot three games without any graphics.

genesisEmu build notes4 min readStory M02-04

The Genesis's Video Display Processor (VDP) is the most complex chip in the console, and rendering its output is a whole milestone (M03). But games interact with it from their first instructions. They configure its registers, upload graphics, poll its status register in tight loops, and organise their main loop around its vertical interrupt. If the VDP doesn't respond correctly, boot code hangs waiting for a bit that never changes, or runs the game loop at the wrong rate.

M02-04 built a VDP that does everything except draw. It turned out to be the larger half of the chip's interface.

Talking to the VDP

The 68000 talks to the VDP through two ports: a data port at $C00000 and a control port at $C00004, mirrored throughout $C00000–$DFFFFF. A write to the control port is either a register write (one word, with 100 in its top bits) or the first half of a two-word command that sets an address and a target: VRAM (64 KB of tiles and tables), CRAM (the 64-colour palette) or VSRAM (vertical scroll values), for reading or writing. After that, data-port accesses stream to or from that memory, with the address advancing by a programmable increment.

The stub implements the command latch, all registers, the three memories and auto-increment. Nothing was rendered, but by the end of boot VRAM was full of exactly what the game had uploaded. The acceptance test checks more than 4,096 non-zero VRAM bytes after 600 frames of Sonic 1. The finer details of the port protocol were completed in M03-01.

Status: where is the beam?

Much of the status register describes timing: are we in vertical blank, in horizontal blank, is this an odd frame, is the FIFO empty? Those bits depend on where the electron beam would be, which the VDP works out from the scanline number and the master-clock position within the line, both supplied by the scheduler (M02-05).

The HV counter at $C00008 reports the beam position directly, and it's not a simple count. Real hardware counters jump at certain values to stay within 8 bits while covering a full line or frame, and games that read them expect the jumps. The vertical counter on NTSC, for example, runs up to $EA and then jumps back to $E5:

The horizontal counter is approximated from the master-clock position within the line: 210 counts per line in the 320-pixel mode (H40), 171 in the 256-pixel mode (H32), with the hardware jumps in both. That approximation is noted in the story, alongside two other simplifications: the FIFO always reads as empty, and the status register's fixed upper bits are hard-wired.

A Genesis game's main loop usually does very little except wait for the vertical interrupt. Get that interrupt wrong and everything else is wrong.

The vertical interrupt

On an NTSC Genesis in 224-line mode, the picture ends after line 223. At line 224 the VDP flags a V-int, and if register 1 has interrupts enabled it raises the 68000's interrupt level to 6. Most of a typical game's per-frame work (reading the pad, copying sprite tables, updating music) runs in that handler, while the main loop just waits for a flag the handler sets.

When the 68000 acknowledges the interrupt, the bus tells the VDP to clear the pending flag. The same line also pulses the Z80's interrupt line for roughly one scanline. The stub implemented the H-int counter too: it counts down after each active line, raises a level-4 interrupt when it underflows, and reloads during vertical blank.

Things that came early

DMA, the VDP's ability to copy memory into VRAM by itself, wasn't in scope, but boot code uses it immediately to upload graphics. So M02-04 implemented it in its simplest form: copies happened instantly, without freezing the 68000, and each one raised the onDma hook for the instrumentation layer. Getting the timing right was left to M03-02, and the story says so.

Tests

Unit tests in tests/unit/vdp_test.cpp parse commands and register writes, check the status bits and counters at known beam positions, and check the interrupt logic. The key acceptance test was a [rom] boot. Over the last 60 of 600 frames, Sonic 1 must take exactly one level-6 interrupt per frame. Sonic 2 manages 58 of 60, which the story records as one lag frame. With a VDP that drew nothing, all three target games reached their main loops and stayed in step with the vertical interrupt.
