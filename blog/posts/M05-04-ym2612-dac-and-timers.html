"SEGA!": the DAC and the timers

Drums, voice clips and the famous start-up chant come from an 8-bit DAC that the Z80 feeds one sample at a time. Sound drivers also depend on the chip's timers and busy flag, which turned out to affect video timing too.

genesisEmu build notes4 min readStory M05-04

Power on Sonic the Hedgehog and the first thing you hear is a voice saying "SEGA!". That isn't FM synthesis. It's a digitised recording, played through the YM2612's DAC mode: when register $2B enables it, channel 6 stops synthesising and instead outputs whatever 8-bit value was last written to register $2A. The Z80 sound driver writes samples to that register one at a time, as fast as it can, which is how Genesis games play drums and voice clips.

M05-04 implemented the DAC and the chip's two timers, which drivers also depend on.

The DAC replaces channel 6

ymfm already models the DAC. genesisEmu's masked output stage from M05-03 had to keep that behaviour, including one detail: the DAC is controlled by both the DAC mute and FM channel 6's mute, since it physically replaces that channel.

The DAC's sample timing is entirely the driver's: the emulator plays each value from the moment it's written. That's why the catch-up rule from M05-03 matters here. A write brings the sound chip up to the writing CPU's exact master clock first, so the spacing between DAC writes, and with it the pitch of the sample, comes out right.

Making silence silent

When the DAC is enabled but idle, its output sits at a constant offset from zero. On a speaker that's inaudible, but in a recording it's a DC offset, and a jump in that offset can be heard as a click. The mixer runs a first-order high-pass filter at 10 Hz, a DC blocker, over the final mix, so the idle DAC settles to true silence:

Once the timers and busy flag were real, the sound drivers waited slightly differently, and that moved the attract-mode video by a few frames.

Timers and the busy flag

The YM2612 has two programmable timers, A and B. When one expires it sets a flag in the status register, and some drivers use those flags to pace their music. The status register also has a busy flag: after each register write, the chip needs a short time before it accepts the next one, and drivers can poll the flag.

ymfm leaves timekeeping to its host through callbacks, so genesisEmu provides the clock. Each generated sample advances time by 144 YM clocks and counts the timers down, carrying any overshoot into the reload:

For a status read to be correct, the chip has to be up to date at the moment of the read. Like writes, status reads first catch the sound chips up to the reading CPU's clock. A unit test checks that timer A sets its status flag.

Until M05, status reads were stubbed to "ready" (M02-03). With a real busy flag and real timers, the drivers' waits changed length, and that shifted attract-mode timing. The M05 pull request records the resulting [golden-update]: three boot state hashes, five frame hashes ("same scenes, checked"), and the scripted-input hashes changed, and the state hash now also covers the sound chips.

Objective checks

The acceptance criteria, a clear "SEGA" voice at the correct pitch and correct S3K drums, are listening tests and still open. The story records the measurements that could be made without ears. In Sonic 1, the "SEGA" chant is the only sound between 2.5 and 4.5 seconds after power-on, at −23 dBFS, with a zero-crossing rate around 1.3 kHz, which is typical of speech. The register log shows 27,000 Z80 writes to $2A in that window. Sonic 2 and Sonic 3 & Knuckles play their SEGA samples at about 4 seconds and 1–2 seconds respectively.

The recordings stayed as scratch files on the build machine, as the project requires for anything taken from the games.
