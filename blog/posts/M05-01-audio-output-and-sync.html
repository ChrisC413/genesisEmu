53,267 samples in, 48,000 out

The Genesis produces sound at an odd native rate, and the emulator's video runs on a different clock from the sound card. A resampler and a feedback loop keep the two in sync without clicks.

genesisEmu build notes4 min readStory M05-01

M05 is the audio milestone. Before any sound chip was emulated, one problem had to be solved: getting samples from the emulator to the speakers smoothly, for hours, without clicks, gaps or drift. That's harder than it sounds, because two clocks are involved that never agree exactly.

Two clocks

The emulated sound hardware produces samples at its own native rate. In genesisEmu that's one stereo sample every 1,008 master clocks, about 53,267 Hz. The host's sound card plays at 48,000 Hz, driven by its own crystal. And the emulator itself is paced by the video: M03-10 runs one emulated frame per display refresh when the display is close to 59.92 Hz.

If the emulator produces audio slightly faster than the sound card plays it, the buffer slowly grows and latency creeps up. Slightly slower, and it eventually runs dry, which you hear as a click. "Slightly" is unavoidable: a 60 Hz display driving a 59.92 Hz console is already 0.13% off, before any crystal error.

Step 1: resample properly

Converting 53,267 Hz to 48,000 Hz isn't a whole-number ratio, so every output sample falls between two input samples. The naive answer, linear interpolation, lets high frequencies fold back as audible aliasing. genesisEmu uses a windowed-sinc resampler, the textbook approach for band-limited conversion:

  • A 32-tap filter kernel: a sinc function (an ideal low-pass filter) shaped by a Blackman window so it can be finite.
  • The cutoff is at 90% of the lower Nyquist frequency, so nothing above about 21.6 kHz survives to alias.
  • The kernel is precomputed at 256 fractional positions ("phases"), and the resampler interpolates between the two nearest, so each output sample costs 32 multiply-adds per channel.

A unit test feeds a 1 kHz tone at the native rate through the resampler to 48 kHz and checks that the pitch stays within 0.5% and the level within 2%.

Neither clock is wrong, and they'll never agree. So the emulator listens to how full the buffer is and nudges its rate by a fraction of a percent.

Step 2: close the loop

The resampler's ratio isn't fixed. Dynamic rate control watches how much audio is queued in the SDL3 audio stream and adjusts the ratio to steer it toward a target of about two frames (33 ms). A fuller queue makes the resampler consume input slightly faster, producing fewer output samples, and an emptier one does the opposite. The adjustment is proportional to the error and clamped to ±0.5%, far too small to hear as a pitch change:

If the queue ever exceeds 250 ms, for example after the window was dragged and the main loop stalled, it's dropped rather than played late. A short gap is better than permanent lag.

This is also what makes the vsync-driven video from M03-10 work. The 0.08 Hz difference between a 60 Hz display and a 59.92 Hz console was deliberately left for audio rate control to absorb.

Pause, fast-forward and no sound card

When emulation pauses, the queued audio is cleared and the device paused, so the last buffer doesn't keep buzzing. When fast-forward runs (M03-10), audio is muted, since a 27× speed-up of the soundtrack isn't useful. And when no audio device can be opened, as on GitHub's CI runners, the app logs a warning and runs silently instead of failing.

What's still open

Both acceptance criteria are listening or soak tests: no underruns over 30 minutes at 60 Hz and at 144 Hz host refresh, and silence immediately on pause. The build machine's display runs at 100 Hz, so the 60 and 144 Hz cases weren't available, and there's no unattended way to listen for an underrun. The story is marked "Implemented; listening / long-run checks pending (Chris)", with both boxes unchecked.
